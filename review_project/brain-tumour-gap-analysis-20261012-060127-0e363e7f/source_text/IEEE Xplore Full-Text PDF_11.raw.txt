Xinyue Zhang , Ali Bahri, Christian Desrosiers , Hui Liu , and Fangxun Bao
Abstract—The accurate segmentation of brain tumors
plays an important role in clinical diagnosis and treatment.
Multimodal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can provide
rich and complementary information for accurate brain tu-
mor segmentation. However, the common problems of in-
complete modalities and small samples in clinical practice
seriously affect the performance of multimodal segmen-
tation. In this work, we design a new framework, named
M2
SegMamba, using Mamba and Masked Autoencoder net-
works for both supervised and self-supervised learning,
aimed at handling small sample brain tumor segmenta-
tion under various incomplete multimodality settings. We
construct a masking strategy suitable for multimodal brain
tumors to precisely extract image features, which serves as
the foundation for image segmentation. By fully leveraging
the capabilities of the Mamba network, we design a multi-
traversal method to facilitate the interaction between inter-
modal and cross-modal image features. Meanwhile, the in-
troduction of TSmamba in skipping connections efficiently
integrates multimodal features. Auxiliary regularizers are
introduced in both the encoder and decoder to further en-
hance the model’s robustness to incomplete modalities. We
conducted experiments on the BraTS 2018 and BraTS 2020
datasets, and the results demonstrate that our method out-
performs state-of-the-art brain tumor segmentation meth-
ods on most subsets of missing modalities.
Index Terms—Brain tumor segmentation, hetero-modal
learning, state-space model, masked image modeling.

I. INTRODUCTION
SEGMENTING brain tumors like gliomas in multimodal
MRI, which involves distinguishing cancerous regions—
such as the whole tumor, core tumor, and enhanced tumor—from
healthy brain tissue, is a vital step for diagnosis and treatment.
However, the inherent heterogeneity of tumors, in terms of
appearance, shape, and varying intensity profiles across mul-
timodal MRI scans (e.g., Fig. 1), poses a significant challenge
for this task [1]. Furthermore, due to differences in scanning
protocols, and patient-related limitations, it is often impossible
to obtain a complete set of MRI sequences for segmentation.
This has prompted researchers to develop hetero-modal learning
methods [2], [3], [4], [5], [6] which can handle cases where any
subset of modalities is not available at inference.
In recent years, Vision Transformers (ViTs) have gained in-
creasing popularity for their ability to model global interactions
in images and seamlessly integrate different input modalities.
Unlikeconvolutionalneuralnetworks(CNN)whichextractlocal
features using convolution filters, ViTs represent images as a
set of patch tokens and combine information across tokens via
their self-attention mechanism [7]. Several studies in medical
imaging have explored the potential of ViTs for multimodal
brain tumor segmentation. Zhang et al. [3] proposed a hybrid
architecture called mmFormer, where available modalities are
first processed separately by a sequence of CNN and ViT blocks,
and then combined with a modality-correlated encoder based
on self-attention. Li et al. [8] trained a CNN with deformable
convolutions and cross-attention to extract and combine features
from a potentially incomplete set of modalities. The proposed
model, named SRMNet, comprises two jointly-trained decoders,
one predicting the segmentation mask of tumor regions, and the
other reconstructing input images. In their M2
FTrans method,
Shi et al. [9] enhanced cross-modality interaction and robustness
to missing modalities by introducing learnable fusion tokens and
computing masked self-attention.
Although the self-attention mechanism in ViTs enables mod-
eling global dependencies in images, its quadratic complexity
with respect to the number of tokens restricts its scalability for
large inputs. This challenge is especially critical in multimodal
segmentation where processing multiple 3D scans is required.
State Space Model (SSM) [10], [11], [12] have recently emerged
as an efficient alternative to Transformers, offering a unique
capability to model global relationships with linear complexity.

The Mamba model [13], in particular, has demonstrated signif-
icant promise across various visual understanding tasks [14],
[15], [16], [17]. Although recent studies have explored the
effectiveness of Mamba for medical image segmentation [18],
[19], [20], [21], its application to hetero-modal scenarios with
missing modalities remains unexplored.
In this paper, we bridge this gap by introducing the first
Mamba-based approach for multimodal brain segmentation,
capable of handling missing modalities. Our method harnesses
the efficiency of SSMs like Mamba to integrate information from
multiple 3D scans while minimizing computational and memory
overhead. Furthermore, to address the challenge of obtaining
expert annotations for medical image segmentation, we propose
a self-supervised learning strategy, which significantly reduces
the need for labeled data during training. Using this strategy, our
method achieves state-of-art performance across scenarios with
varying degrees of labeled data scarcity.
The main contributions of our work are listed as follows:
1) We present M2
SegMamba, the first Mamba-based
method for 3D multimodal image segmentation with
missing modalities. Our method effectively captures
global interactions within entire images and across dif-
ferent modalities, while minimizing computational and
memory requirements.
2) We introduce a novel self-supervised pre-training strategy
that utilizes image masking to enhance the robustness of
our method, particularly when only a limited number of
labeled samples are available. To the best of our knowl-
edge, this is the first study to investigate this strategy
within a Mamba-based segmentation model.
3) We assess our M2
SegMamba method through compre-
hensive experiments across a diverse set of scenarios,
varying in available modalities and amounts of labeled
data. Our findings highlight the robustness and superior
performance of M2
SegMamba in comparison to existing
approaches.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Multimodal Brain Tumor Segmentation
The methods proposed for this fundamental task in medical
image analysis can be broadly categorized into two types: those
that require the complete set of modalities and those capable of
handling missing modalities. Methods in the first category, typ-
ically based on CNNs [22], [23] or ViTs [24], [25], [26], adhere
to the conventional image segmentation pipeline, maintaining
the same input during both training and inference. Conversely,
the second category of approaches tackles the challenge of
hetero-modal learning, where any subset of modalities may be
missing during training or inference. A solution to this prob-
lem uses knowledge distillation to transfer the knowledge of a
model trained with the full set of modalities to one that takes a
subset of these modalities as input [6], [27], [28]. However, this
approach can be costly to implement in practice, as a separate
model is needed for each possible subset of missing modalities.
Another strategy employs a generative model to synthesize the
missing modalities. For example, Huang et al. [29] proposed a
context-aware generative adversarial network (CoCa-GAN),
based on an encoder-decoder architecture, where the decoder
generates the missing target modality. One limitation of this
strategy is that it requires training a separate generative network
for each modality and is highly sensitive to the quality of the
generated images.
Rather than learning modality-specific networks, another
group of methods focuses on developing a unified model ca-
pable of directly handling missing modalities. For example, the
HeMIS model by Havaei et al. [2] integrates feature embed-
dings from an arbitrary set of modalities by calculating their
mean and variance. Ding et al. [5] proposed the Region-aware
Fusion Network (RFNet), which learns a probability map to
segment multimodal features into distinct regions and applies
attention weights within each region to dynamically adjust the
contributions of different image modalities.
B. State Space Models
State Space Models (SSMs) [30] have gained increasing
attention as sequence-to-sequence models for their ability to
efficiently capture long-range dependencies while supporting
parallel training. Compared to the self-attention mechanism in
Transformers, which computes an attention weight for each
pair of input tokens, SSMs model the interdependence between
tokens through latent state variables that are updated sequen-
tially. Mamba [13], a widely used SSM, enhanced the struc-
tured state-space sequence (S4) model [31] by incorporating
a data-dependent selection mechanism. Adapting this model
to vision tasks, VMamba [32] designed a Cross-Scan Module
(CSM) that extends 1D selective scanning to the domain of 2D
images. Similarly, Vision Mamba [33] introduced Vim blocks
that combine positional embeddings with bidirectional sequence
modeling. CU-Mamba [34] proposed integrating a dual state
space model framework into U-Net structure. It also adopted a
Spatial SSM module for global context encoding as well as a
Channel SSM component to retain channel-related features.
Recent studies have introduced SSM-based models for med-
ical image segmentation. Xing et al. [18] proposed SegMamba,
an innovative 3D image segmentation model that efficiently
captureslong-rangedependenciesacrosswhole-volumefeatures
at multiple scales. Ma et al. [35] developed a hybrid CNN-SSM
block, which combines the local feature extraction power of
convolutional layers with the long-range dependency modeling
capabilities of SSMs, enabling its application to both 2D and
3D biomedical image segmentation. Our work builds upon
these previous studies by introducing the first Mamba-based
approach for 3D multimodal image segmentation in the presence
of missing modalities.
C. Masked Image Modeling
Masked image modeling (MIM) is a self-supervised learning
technique in computer vision that trains models by masking parts
of the input—such as pixels, patches, or latent representations—
and using the remaining visible context to predict the masked
regions and learn meaningful representations [36]. Research
in MIM primarily explores two approaches: comparing latent

plete multimodal MRI data. By reconstructing masked segments
of the network’s latent features, our method efficiently learns a
more robust representation of multimodal images.
III. METHOD
Our Mamba-based approach for multimodal brain tumor seg-
mentation with missing modalities comprises two key steps: 1)
a pre-training phase focused on masked feature reconstruction
(Fig. 2), and 2) a fine-tuning phase for optimizing the segmen-
tation task (Fig. 4). Before outlining these two steps, we first
provide some background information on Mamba, which serves
as the context modeling component in our method.

B. Masked Image Reconstruction Pre-Training
As shown in Fig. 2, we utilize a 3D autoencoder architecture
in the pre-training stage to capture both local and contextual
features through multimodal image reconstruction. Unlike stan-
dard autoencoders, which consist of a symmetric encoder and
decoder, our architecture has a dedicated encoder for each
modality. These modality-specific encoders integrate convo-
lutional and Mamba blocks to effectively capture both local
and global interactions within and across multimodal images,
ensuring robust performance even in the presence of missing
modalities.
The features extracted by encoders are combined using our
proposed multimodal traversal strategy, which processes 3D
patches in different sequential orders accounting for both spatial
position and modality. This enables the model to learn intra- and
inter-modality dependencies between patches. For efficiency, a
single convolutional decoder is employed to reconstruct the im-
ages across all modalities, including missing ones, from the out-
puts of multimodal traversals. Finally, to capture robust features
that encode structural information across multiple modalities,
we employ a multimodal masking strategy that randomly masks
patch features both within and across different image modalities.
The following subsections provide a detailed explanation of
these components of our method.
1) Modality-Specific Image Encoder: As shown in Fig. 2, we
employ a hybrid architecture for the modality-specific encoders,
integrating a 3D convolutional head to extract local context
features from 3D images and a Mamba tail to capture global
dependencies across 3D patches.

propose a cross-modal masking strategy which simultaneously
masks the same token (i.e., corresponding to the same patch
position) across all modalities.

C. Segmentation Fine-Tuning
The self-supervised pre-training step, outlined in
Section III-B, leverages contextual and structural information
to extract meaningful features, enabling effective learning of the
downstream segmentation task with minimal annotated data. In
this section, we detail how the pre-trained encoder is utilized to
construct the final network for brain tumor image segmentation.
1) Segmentation Network: As shown in Fig. 4, several im-
portant changes are made to the architecture in the segmentation
fine-tuning step. First, the reconstruction decoder is replaced
by a segmentation decoder Dseg which is also composed of
convolutional blocks. Following segmentation networks like 3D
U-Net [43], we also introduce skip connections between the
encoder and decoder to preserve fine details for segmentation.
In our architecture, each modality has a separate encoder,
while a single decoder processes the combined features. This
setup prevents the use of standard skip connections designed for
symmetric encoder-decoder models. We solve this problem us-
ing tri-orientated spatial Mamba (TSMamba) blocks [18] in each
layer encoder output feature fusion, which effectively integrate
features from different modalities within each skip connection.
A TSMamba block consists of a gated spatial convolution mod-
ule (GSC), a tri-orientated Mamba module (ToM), and an MLP
layer. The GSC module transforms this input using convolution
blocks and a residual connection. The ToM module then tiles
the 3D input features into three sequences, corresponding to the
forward (zf ), reverse (zr), and inter-slice (zs) directions, and
processes each of them with Mamba.
2) Auxiliary Regularizers: In brain tumor segmentation,
some MRI sequences, such as FLAIR, provide more discrim-
inative information than others. Consequently, the model may
over-rely on these modalities, leading to significant accuracy
degradation when they are unavailable. To mitigate this and pro-
mote balanced learning across all modalities, as shown in Fig. 4,
we incorporate auxiliary regularizers inspired by those in [3].
To regularize separate convolutional encoders, we first up-
sample their outputs using a shared decoder having the same
architecture as the convolutional decoder at the end of our net-
work. We then encourage the segmentation for each modality m
to match the ground-truth labels using a regularization loss Lm
reg.
Similarly, we regularize the decoder of our model by progres-
sively upsampling the feature maps in each of its layer through
deconvolution, and then obtaining dense predictions from the
upsampled features with an additional softmax layer. The deep
supervision loss for layer , denoted as L
DS, measures the error
between the ground truth segmentation and the prediction at this
layer.

IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS
A. Dataset and Implementation
1) Dataset: The experiments were conducted using two
datasets of the multimodal Brain Tumor Segmentation (BraTS)
challenge [44]: BraTS 2018 and BraTS 2020. These datasets
comprise four MRI modalities: Flair, T1c, T1, and T2. The
BraTS 2018 dataset includes 190 training cases and 95 valida-
tion cases, while the BraTS 2020 dataset contains 246 training
cases and 123 validation cases. To simulate a few-shot learning
scenario, the segmentation model training was performed using
10% of the available training data (results for different training
data ratios are reported in Table V). All images from each
modality were preprocessed by normalizing and resizing them
to a uniform resolution of 128 × 128 × 128. The performance
of the proposed method was evaluated based on segmentation
results for the whole tumor (WT), tumor core (TC), and en-
hancing tumor (ET) regions. The Dice similarity coefficient
was employed as the primary metric to quantitatively assess
segmentation accuracy.
2) Implementation Details: All images in the training set
(100%) are used for the unsupervised pre-training step, ignoring
the labels of these images. Optimization was performed with
Adam with a learning rate of 1 × 10−4
, a batch size of 2, and a
total of 300 training epochs. For fine-tuning, we used an initial
learning rate of 2 × 10−4
, a weight decay of 1 × 10−4
, a batch
size of 1, and a total of 1,000 training epochs.
For the main experiments, we set the mask ratio empirically
to γ = 87.5%. The impact of this ratio is later investigated in
Section IV-C2.

V. DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION
To address the problems of missing modalities and few-shot
scenarios in clinical practice, we propose a novel Mamba-based
framework named M2
SegMamba for few-shot and incomplete
multimodal brain tumor image segmentation. Our approach
innovatively integrates Masked Image Modeling with 3D mul-
timodal brain tumor segmentation, showing great potential in
small-sample scenarios. Leveraging the advantages of Mamba
for long-sequence modeling, we design Mamba-based network,
which further enhance the segmentation accuracy of the net-
work. Compared to Transformer-based methods, our approach
maintains excellent inference efficiency. Experiments on the
BraTS 2018 and BraTS 2020 datasets validate the effectiveness
of our proposed method. Clinically, M2
SegMamba’s ability to
perform with limited data and incomplete modalities highlights
its potential as a practical tool for real-world applications, espe-
cially in resource-constrained healthcare environments.
While our proposed M2
SegMamba framework has demon-
strated robust performance in the challenging settings of few-
shot and incomplete multimodal segmentation tasks, the model
still exhibits certain performance variations under specific con-
ditions, highlighting areas for potential improvement. For in-
stance, segmentation accuracy may vary across different tumor
subregions when critical imaging modalities are absent. These
differences likely stem from the inherent heterogeneity of tumor
structures and the diverse information content across imaging
modalities. In addition, while this study focuses on MRI-based
segmentation, the modular architecture and adaptive multimodal
traversal strategy of M2
SegMamba indicate its potential for
