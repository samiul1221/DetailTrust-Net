A Foundation Model for Lesion Segmentation on
Brain MRI With Mixture of Modality Experts
Xinru Zhang , Ni Ou , Berke Doga Basaran, Marco Visentin, Mengyun Qiao , Renyang Gu,
Paul M. Matthews , Yaou Liu, Chuyang Ye , and Wenjia Bai , Senior Member, IEEE
Abstract—Brain lesion segmentation is crucial for neuro-
logical disease research and diagnosis. As different types of
lesions exhibit distinct characteristics on different imaging
modalities, segmentation methods are typically developed
in a task-specific manner, where each segmentation model
is tailored to a specific lesion type and modality. However,
the use of task-specific models requires predetermination
of the lesion type and imaging modality, which compli-
cates their deployment in real-world scenarios. In this work,
we propose a universal foundation model for brain lesion
segmentation on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which
can automatically segment different types of brain lesions
given input of various MRI modalities. We develop a novel
Mixture of Modality Experts (MoME) framework with multiple
expert networks attending to different imaging modalities.
A hierarchical gating network is proposed to combine the
expert predictions and foster expertise collaboration. More-
over, to avoid the degeneration of each expert network,
we introduce a curriculum learning strategy during training
to preserve the specialisation of each expert. In addition to

I. INTRODUCTION
BRAIN lesion segmentation plays an essential role in
neurological research and diagnosis by offering a quan-
titative description of pathologies [1], [2]. Deep learning has
greatly advanced the progress of brain lesion segmentation
in recent years [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]. Since brain lesions
tend to manifest with different characteristics due to various
patient-dependent pathological alterations, the segmentation of
different types of brain lesions requires the use of different
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) modalities [9], [10], [11],
such as T1-weighted (T1w), T2-weighted (T2w), T1 contrast-
enhanced (T1ce), FLAIR, diffusion weighted imaging (DWI),
etc. They are either used standalone or as a combination [12],
[13], [14], which increases the complexity of segmentation
model development. In the traditional paradigm (Fig. 1a), task-
specific models are developed, where each model segments
brain lesions on a specific modality or modality combi-
nation [2], [15]. In addition, for different types of brain
lesions, such as stroke lesions, white-matter hyperintensities
(WMH), glioma, etc., different task-specific models need to
be developed. At the deployment stage, among all these task-
specific models, clinicians are compelled to manually choose
the optimal model for each patient MRI scan. This process
hinders the practical deployment of artificial intelligence (AI)
models. Contrary to this traditional paradigm, we propose a
novel task-agnostic universal foundational model (Fig. 1b),
which is capable of automatically segmenting various types
of brain lesions regardless of the input MRI modality. The
model can also be extended to handle heterogeneous inputs
with multiple modalities.
Foundation models have gained popularity in medical image
segmentation, thanks to the success of the Segment Anything
Model (SAM) [16] and its variants [17], [18], [19], [20],
as well as other innovative foundation models specifically
developed for medical imaging [21], [22], [23], [24]. Earlier

focusing on narrower scopes are explored, which are designed
for a particular modality or a particular organ/task [25].
For example, several models are proposed specifically for
computed tomography (CT) image segmentation [22], [26],
[27], showing promising results for anatomical structure and
large tumour segmentation. Despite the progress, most existing
foundation models may not be directly applicable to brain

addressed by many of the existing foundation models.
In this study, inspired by Mixture of Experts (MoE) [28],
[29], [30], we propose a Mixture of Modality Experts (MoME)
foundational model for brain lesion segmentation on MRI,
illustrated in Fig. 1c. Our main contributions are summarised
as follows:
• MoME is developed to be task-agnostic and capable of
handling diverse imaging modalities and accommodating
various types of brain lesions. Each expert specialises in
processing certain image modalities.
• To explore collaboration among the experts, a hierarchical
gating network is designed to combine expert predictions
and facilitate collaborative expertise exploration layer by
layer. Additionally, to keep the specialisability of each
expert, an anti-degeneration strategy based on curriculum
learning is developed.
• We conducted comprehensive experiments on nine brain
imaging datasets comprising a total number of 6,585
annotated 3D images, with five MRI modalities and eight
lesion types. The results show that MoME outperforms
competing foundation models in multiple aspects and
provides improved generalisation onto unseen datasets.
A preliminary version of this work has been published in
MICCAI 2024 [31], focusing on using a single image as input.
Here, we have substantially extended the conference version.
The MoME model has been extended to MoME+ to handle
combined multiple modalities as input. To this end, we develop
a novel dispatch network with a soft assignment strategy and
prior constraints, and employ random dropout of modalities
during training to mimic the heterogeneous input. When a
maximum of N modalities are available, a single unified
MoME+ achieves segmentation performance comparable to
that of 2N − 1 dedicated nnU-Nets for different modal-
ity combinations. Additionally, we include new experimental
results for handling missing modalities, investigate strategies
of decoder sharing for multiple experts to improve model
efficiency, as well as extend the illustration and discussion.
The proposed MoME and MoME+ models demonstrate
promising segmentation performance that is agnostic against
modalities and modality combinations. Since only a single
universal model is needed, it eases model management and
deployment, making it applicable to a broader range of
scenarios. The code of MoME and MoME+ is available at
https://github.com/ZhangxinruBIT/MoME.
II. RELATED WORK
A. Brain Lesion Segmentation and Medical Imaging
Foundation Models
The task of brain lesion segmentation differs significantly
from natural image segmentation or other anatomical structure
segmentation tasks. Unlike anatomical structure segmentation,
brain lesion segmentation deals with a broader variations of
data distributions, in terms of different lesion types, varying
spatial locations, scattering of multiple lesions, and irregular
shapes [1], [32], which poses substantial challenges for devel-
oping segmentation foundation models.
Reference [23] proposed a universal medical image seg-
mentation model, Hermes, which allows modality information
and target segmented objects to be embedded as context
priors into the model. However, brain lesion types and their
locations are often patient-specific, which are difficult to be
embedded as context priors. SAM-inspired models such as
SAM-Med3D [17] and MedSAM [19] have demonstrated the

potential of foundational segmentation models for 3D medical
image segmentation. These models require input prompts in
the format of click points or bounding boxes. While prompt
generation is relatively easy for a single large tumour that
appears as a central cluster in the brain, e.g., glioma, it can
be more challenging to define for other types of lesions,
e.g., stroke lesions that manifest as small and challenging-to-
detect anomalies (see Fig. 1a) [2]. Additionally, white matter
hyperintensity (WMH) are often widely dispersed [5], [33] and
scattered across the brain at multiple locations (see Fig. 1a),
which can be easily missed by point-based or bounding box-
based prompts. Neuralizer [4] proposed a different paradigm
for developing medical imaging foundational models by lever-
aging a context set. After the model pre-training stage, the
model generalises to a new task by learning transferable
features from a context set that contains paired images and
segmentation masks, similar to few-shot learning. Neuralizer
is mainly evaluated on 2D coronal slices instead of 3D
volumes, possibly due to the computational resource limitation
in learning from a 3D context set.
B. Mixture of Experts
The mixture of experts (MoE) approach is widely acknowl-
edged for its efficacy in scaling up foundation models and
improving their performance [34], [35], [36], particularly in
handling larger models and datasets with strong generali-
sation capabilities [37], [38], [39]. MoE involves creating
multiple distinct experts tasked with learning both shared
and task-specific knowledge, while employing a gating func-
tion to regulate the contribution of each expert. MoE has
been explored for applications in natural language processing
(NLP) [40], [41], multi-modal learning [30], [42], and com-
puter vision [28], [43], [44], [45]. In computer vision, MoE
has been demonstrated effective in image classification [28],
[43] and segmentation tasks [28], [44]. In the field of med-
ical image analysis, Patcher [28] regarded the feature maps
extracted from different resolution levels as expert outputs and
introduced a MoE decoder to integrate multi-level global-to-
fine features. Here, we propose that the definition of experts
in the MoE model could be carefully tailored to specific
tasks and data domains. Brain lesion segmentation involves
multiple imaging modalities. Several studies have shown that
utilising the modality information benefits the segmentation
performance [46], [47]. There is potential to leverage the
characteristics of each modality to define the expert for each
modality under the MoE framework.
C. Heterogeneous Input With Missing Modalities
In the field of medical imaging, the input data are inherently
heterogeneous [48], with various imaging modalities or com-
bination of modalities. A common issue that requires attention
is the modality missing problem, where one or several of
the multiple imaging modalities are missing [12], [13], [14],
[46], [47], [49]. To address this issue, strategies such as
synthesizing missing modalities using a generative model [50]
or learning a modality-invariant feature space [46], [51], [52]
have been explored. If N imaging modalities are needed for a
segmentation task and some modalities are missing, the total
number of combinations of remaining available modalities is
2N − 1. Consequently, numerous input-specific segmentation
models need to be trained, dedicated to each modality combi-
nation [14], [53]. Wang et al. proposed non-dedicated training,
which results in N models and reduces the model complex-
ity [12]. The non-dedicated training approach, while requiring
fewer models for training, may compromise performance to
some extent compared to dedicated training [12]. In this work,
we explore a different approach, which is to develop a single
foundation model capable of handling different modality com-
binations. Such a foundation model would offer convenience
and ease for model development and deployment.
III. METHODS
A. Method Overview
The architecture of MoME features two major components,
namely the expert networks and the gating network. Each
expert network is specialised in a specific domain of imaging
modality. The gating network acts as a decision-maker, ensem-
bling the expert network outputs and dynamically adjusting the
ensemble weights with hierarchical guidance. While training
the segmentation model, an anti-degeneration strategy based
on curriculum learning is employed, which enables the model
training to gradually transition from specialising the role of
each expert to determining the entire model through collab-
oration. We develop our method for two types of input data,
only a single but any modality as input (MoME, Sec. III-B)
and a combination of multiple modalities as input (MoME+,
Sec. III-C).
B. MoME for Single Input Image

regardless of its imaging modality. We focus on the five most
widely used brain MRI modalities, including T1w, T2w, T1ce,
FLAIR, and DWI, and thus N = 5. To address the problem of
modality diversity, in MoME, N expert networks {Ei }N
i=1 are

Without losing generality, we assume that each expert network
follows a multi-resolution encoder-decoder architecture, which
is common for medical image segmentation such as in U-
Net [54]. Note that the experts here are built for 3D brain
image segmentation, distinguishing them from sparse MoE
architectures for 2D natural images where a higher number
of experts can be afforded [55], [56].

ensemble among the expert outputs. Note that different

C. MoME+: Extended MoME for Multiple Input Images
The previously described MoME model accepts a single
input image, regardless of its modality. The single input image
goes through multiple experts and their predictions are ensem-
bled. Here we extend its capability to handle multi-modality
input images, which are common in brain lesion segmentation,
and the number of modalities and their combinations can
vary. We refer to this extended model as MoME+ hereinafter.
In MoME+, we still have N expert networks, with N denoting
the maximum number of modalities. There are multiple input
images, the number of which can be smaller than N due
to missing modalities. To address the mismatch between
the number of available input images and the number of
expert networks, and to leverage information from available
modalities to infer channels for missing modalities, we develop
an additional dispatch network D, which generates a N × N
dispatch matrix, and then dispatches the input images to the
N expert networks.

D. Implementation Details
We implement each expert network and the gating network
using the nnU-Net architecture [54], which is a strong segmen-
tation backbone as shown in [22] and [54]. Additionally, all
networks employ deep supervision [54]. The dispatch network
for the multi-input MoME+ model utilises three layers of fully
connected layers to achieve its lightweight task, where the
numbers of channels are 4, 8, and 16 sequentially, and the
activation functions are all ReLU [59]. As each expert network
is expected to specialise in one imaging modality, the expert
networks are first trained separately using a combined soft
Dice and cross-entropy loss function to acquire the modality
knowledge. Subsequently, the gating network is trained with
the expert networks being fine-tuned using the curriculum
learning loss in Eq. (2) for single modality input and Eq. (7)
for heterogeneous multi-modality input respectively. All the
hyperparameters (including {kl}L
l=1) follow the default setting
in nnU-Net [54], except that 1) a lower learning rate of 1e−3
is set for the dynamic curriculum learning loss [38] and 2) the
number of training epochs is set to 800, a sufficient number
for convergence.

B. Competing Task-Specific and Foundation Models
MoME was compared with the following task-specific and
foundation models for single modality input settings.
Task-specific nnU-Nets: The nine datasets cover a variety
of brain lesions and modalities. We regard the segmentation of
each modality for each dataset as a distinct task [24], resulting
in a total number of 17 tasks (14 tasks for seen datasets and
3 tasks for unseen datasets). Thus, 17 supervised task-specific
nnU-Net models were trained.
nnU-Net: A single nnU-Net [54] was trained with the
combined training sets from all public datasets. It can be
viewed as a baseline foundation model.
Multi-Talent [22]: Multi-Talent demonstrates superior
performance in handling conflicting class definitions for
multi-dataset training and shows promising lesion segmenta-
tion results. We trained it with the combined training sets.
Hermes [23]: Hermes is a foundation model that injects

SAM-Med3D [17]: This is a SAM-based [16] model trained

For heterogeneous multi-modality input, the competing
foundational models cannot be applied. We compared the
MoME+ model with 2N −1 dedicated nnU-Nets, each trained
for one combination of modalities. For the BraTS2021 dataset,
N = 4 modalities were involved.
All models were trained for 800 epochs on Nvidia
A100 GPUs. We used the default hyperparameters for the
transformer-based SAM-Med3D. All the other models were
based on nnU-Net and used its default hyperparameters.

V. DISCUSSION
Foundational models have gained popularity and developed
rapidly in the natural image domain. However, they do not
account for the complexity of medical imaging modalities.
In this paper, MoME and MoME+ have been proposed
with the aim of creating a task-agnostic model capable of

ZHANG et al.: FOUNDATION MODEL FOR LESION SEGMENTATION ON BRAIN MRI WITH MoME 2603
handling various modalities and lesion types in clinical brain
MRI, regardless of the modality or modality combinations.
The diversity of imaging modalities is unique to the field
of medical imaging, presenting challenges in developing a
unified foundational model. However, this complexity also
offers a unique opportunity to advance foundational model
development by exploiting the complementary relationships
between modalities. This motivates us to choose the Mixture
of Experts (MoE) architecture and regard each modality as an
expert within this framework.
MoME demonstrates its clinical potential by serving as
a unified foundation model for brain lesion segmentation,
achieving comparable performance to numerous task-specific
nnU-Nets. This approach enhances user-friendliness for clin-
icians by eliminating the need to train and manage multiple
dedicated models tailored to a specific modality and lesion
type. In practical settings, the variability of patient imag-
ing modalities and lesion types makes managing numerous
task-specific models impractical and burdensome within
a hospital’s neuroimaging department. Adopting a unified
foundation model like MoME simplifies this complexity,
thereby improving clinical workflow efficiency. Furthermore,
we extend MoME from a single input scenario to MoME+ to
address the multi-input scenario, with potential missing modal-
ities and heterogeneous combinations of input modalities. This
is a challenge not typically addressed by existing foundation
models designed for natural images. Through innovations like
the soft dispatch network and embedding modality information
into the gating network, we demonstrate the capability of
MoME+ to manage diverse inputs with the potential to handle
more complex scenarios in clinical neuroimaging. Addition-
ally, although MoME is specifically designed for brain lesion
segmentation, it is feasible to extend this approach to a
wider range of applications. For instance, it could be applied
to organ segmentation tasks or other domains that involve
multiple modality inputs, where the collaboration between
modality-based experts may benefit the task performance.
At the moment, most publicly available and in-house
datasets annotate only one type of lesion per brain image.
However, for some patients, more than one lesion types
can exist in the same brain image, and not all of these
lesion types are annotated in the current datasets. Improving
the dataset annotation accuracy could be achieved in future
work by implementing quality control measures or employing
advanced data engines [16]. Furthermore, we can generate
additional training samples using lesion-aware data augmenta-
tion techniques such as CarveMix [2], [68]. This method can
generate augmented brain images mixed with multiple types
of lesions, along with augmented annotations. This will enrich
the training datasets to encompass more diverse variations of
brain lesion types, which can further improve the foundation
model for brain lesion segmentation.
VI. CONCLUSION
We have developed a novel universal foundation model
MoME for brain lesion segmentation on MRI, where a mixture
of modality experts is incorporated to address the challenge
of modality diversity. MoME enables collaboration between
experts with hierarchical gating and mitigates expert degenera-
tion with a curriculum learning strategy. Additionally, we have
extended MoME to MoME+ with soft routing dispatch,
allowing it to handle heterogeneous multi-modality inputs.
Extensive experimental results on nine public and in-house
datasets and 17 tasks show that it outperforms competing
foundation models across different modalities and lesion types.
It is also more efficient than task-specific models, providing a
flexible way for model deployment.
