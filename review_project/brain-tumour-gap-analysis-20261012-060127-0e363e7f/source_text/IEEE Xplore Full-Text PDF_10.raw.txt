DCTC-Net: Dual-Branch Cross-Fusion
Transformer–CNN Architecture for
Medical Image Segmentation
Rui Sun
Abstract—Hybrid architectures that combine convolutional
neural networks (CNNs) with Transformers have emerged as
a promising approach for medical image segmentation. How-
ever, existing networks based on this hybrid architecture often
encounter two challenges. First, while the CNN branch effectively
captures local image features through convolution operations,
vanilla convolution lacks the ability to achieve adaptive fea-
ture extraction. Second, although the Transformer branch can
model global image information, conventional self-attention (SA)
primarily focuses on spatial relationships, neglecting channel
and cross-dimensional attention, leading to suboptimal segmen-
tation results, particularly for medical images with complex
backgrounds. To address these limitations, we propose a dual-
branch cross-fusion Transformer–CNN architecture for medical
image segmentation (DCTC-Net). Our network provides two key
advantages. First, a dynamic deformable convolution (DDConv)
is integrated into the CNN branch to overcome the limitations
of adaptive feature extraction with fixed-size convolution ker-
nels and also eliminate the issue of shared convolution kernel
parameters across different inputs, significantly enhancing the
feature expression capabilities of the CNN branch. Second,
a (shifted)-window adaptive complementary attention module
((S)W-ACAM) and compact convolutional projection are incor-
porated into the Transformer branch, enabling the network to
comprehensively learn cross-dimensional long-range dependen-
cies in medical images. Experimental results demonstrate that the
proposed DCTC-Net achieves superior medical image segmenta-
tion performance compared to state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods,
including CNN and Transformer networks. In addition, our
DCTC-Net requires fewer parameters and lower computational
costs and does not rely on pretraining.
Index Terms—Deep learning, dynamic deformable convolution
(DDConv), medical image segmentation, (shifted)-window adap-
tive complementary attention.

medical image into distinct regions with unique char-
acteristics. Accurate medical image segmentation can not
only facilitate abnormal detection in human body regions but
also guide clinicians. Consequently, precise medical image
segmentation has become a key component of computer-aided

high-density noise, low contrast, and blurred edges. Thus,
the challenge of rapidly and accurately segmenting specific
human organs and lesions from medical images remains a
major hurdle in the field of smart medicine.
Traditional methods for medical image segmentation ini-
tially relied on handcrafted features developed by medical
experts based on their domain expertise [3]. These methods,
while possessing a strong mathematical basis and theoretical
support, often suffer from poor generalization abilities when
applied to different organs or lesions in the human body. Later,
inspired by fully convolutional networks (FCNs) [4] and the
encoder–decoder paradigm, Ronneberger et al. [1] designed
the U-Net, which was first applied to medical image segmen-
tation. Since then, the U-shaped encoder–decoder structure
has garnered widespread attention. The small parameter count
and effective segmentation performance of the U-Net have
enabled deep learning to achieve breakthroughs in the field
of medical image segmentation. Subsequently, a variety of
improved medical image segmentation networks have been
developed, such as 2-D U-Net++ [5], ResDO-UNet [6], SGU-
Net [7], 2.5-D RIU-Net [8], 3-D Unet [9], and V-Net [10]. The
rapid advancement of convolutional neural networks (CNNs)
in medical image segmentation is mainly attributable to the
scale invariance and inductive bias inherent in the convolution
operation. However, the fixed receptive field limits CNNs’
ability to effectively capture long-range spatial dependencies
among pixels in medical images.
To address the limitations of CNNs in capturing global
dependencies, Vaswani et al. [11] proposed the Transformer
architecture originally for natural language processing (NLP),
which was later adapted for image analysis. By leveraging
self-attention (SA) mechanisms and long-range dependence
modeling, Transformers effectively overcome the problem of
CNNs being limited to local feature extraction. Numerous
Transformer-based methods have been applied to medical
image segmentation, including Swin-Unet [12], BAT [13],
Swin UNETR [14], and UCTransNet [15]. These methods
can be broadly categorized into pure Transformer architectures
and hybrid CNN-Transformer architectures. Pure Transformer
architectures rely on SA to model long-range dependencies.

CNN-Transformer architectures leverage the complementary

suffer from two main limitations. First, these networks often

features, resulting in inadequate SA expression. To address
the aforementioned problems, our main contributions are as
follows.
1) We propose a novel dynamic deformable convolution
(DDConv) module for medical image feature extraction.
Leveraging task-adaptive learning, DDConv flexibly
adjusts both the weight coefficients and deformation
offsets of the convolution operation adaptively. DDConv
overcomes the limitations of fixed, narrow receptive
fields and nonadaptive convolution kernel parameters
that are prevalent in vanilla convolution and its variants,
such as dilated convolution and involution. Conse-
quently, DDConv enhances the network’s ability to
detect subtle lesions and targets exhibiting significant
scale variations in medical images.
2) We introduce a novel (shifted)-window adaptive comple-
mentary attention module ((S)W-ACAM). The module
enables cross-dimensional global modeling of medical
images through four parallel branches using adap-
tive weight coefficient learning. In contrast to existing
attention mechanisms, such as CBAM and Nonlocal,
(S)W-ACAM effectively addresses the limitations of
conventional attention by explicitly modeling the cross-
dimensional relationships between spatial features and
channels. This allows (S)W-ACAM to capture cross-
dimensional, long-range correlations within medical
images and enhance the separability between seg-
mented objects and the background. As illustrated in
Fig. 1, by integrating shifted windows and compact
convolutional projection, our (S)W-ACAM significantly
reduces the network’s parameter count and computa-
tional complexity compared to conventional CNNs and
pure Transformer architectures while maintaining the
capability to capture comprehensive global features.
3) We present a novel parallel network structure for
medical image segmentation, termed dual-branch cross-
fusion Transformer-CNN architecture for medical image
segmentation (DCTC-Net), based on adaptive CNNs and
cross-dimensional Transformer architectures. In contrast
to existing hybrid architectures that combine CNNs with
Transformers, such as Swin-Unet [12] and Swin UNETR
[14], DCTC-Net enhances representation learning by
effectively integrating local and global features at var-
ious resolutions through cross-fusion between CNNs
and Transformers. Moreover, DCTC-Net eliminates the
need for pretraining, requires fewer parameters, and
incurs lower computational costs, with only 11.58 M
parameters and 4.53 GFLOPs.
II. RELATED WORK
Medical image segmentation plays a crucial role in the
field of medical image processing and is also one of the
key technologies of computer-aided diagnosis and treatment
systems. In recent years, with the rapid development of deep
learning techniques, researchers have continuously developed
numerous deep network models for medical image segmenta-
tion. Generally, medical image segmentation methods can be
coarsely classified into two categories: CNN-based methods
and Transformer-based methods.
A. CNNs-Based Methods
CNNs have demonstrated remarkable success in medical
image segmentation by learning hierarchical feature repre-
sentations through multilayer architectures. Unlike traditional
handcrafted methods, CNN-based approaches can automati-
cally extract discriminative features from large-scale medical
datasets, making them indispensable components of modern
intelligent medical systems.
Ronneberger et al. [1] drew inspiration from the FCN [4]
and designed the first end-to-end network, U-Net, for medical
image segmentation in the ISBI cell tracking challenge. The

U-Net architecture employs a symmetrical encoder–decoder
structure, enabling the full utilization of local contextual infor-
mation within medical images while reducing the reliance on
extensive training datasets. Consequently, U-Net can achieve
effective medical image segmentation even with limited data.
Building upon U-Net, Alom et al. [17] developed R2U-Net by
integrating U-Net with ResNet [18] and recurrent neural net-
work (RCNN) components [19], achieving strong performance
across multiple medical image segmentation datasets, includ-
ing blood vessels and retinas. Subsequently, Gu et al. [21]
introduced dynamic convolution [20] into U-Net, proposing
CA-Net. Experiments using medical datasets have shown that
CA-Net not only improves the segmentation accuracy of medi-
cal images but also reduces the network training time. Building
on U-Net, Yang et al. [23] incorporated the idea of resid-
ual connections and deformable convolution [22], integrating
residual deformable convolution into U-Net and proposing
DCU-Net. DCU-Net achieves superior segmentation perfor-
mance compared to U-Net on the DRIVE medical dataset.
Lei et al. [7] designed SGU-Net based on U-Net, proposing
an ultralight convolution module and an additional adversarial
shape-constraint that significantly improve the segmentation
accuracy of abdominal medical images through self-supervised
training. Although CNNs have made significant progress in
architecture design, their success is primarily due to scale
invariance and inductive bias in local modeling. However,
fixed receptive fields limit their ability to capture relationships
between distant pixels in medical images and constrain the
capacity to model long-range dependencies.
Compared to existing approaches, such as vanilla con-
volution [1], dynamic convolution [20], and deformable
convolution [22], our DDConv not only adaptively adjusts the
weight coefficient and deformation offsets of the convolution
kernels according to the medical image characteristics, but also
better adapts to the shape of organs and small lesions with
significant scale variations. Consequently, it enhances the local
feature expression ability of the segmentation network.
B. Transformer-Based Methods
Vaswani et al. [11] introduced the Transformer. Due to its
unique architecture, Transformers gained the ability to process
inputs of variable length, establish long-range dependence
modeling, and capture global information. Transformer’s suc-
cess is primarily attributed to the SA mechanism, enabling it
to capture long-range dependencies.
Building on the Transformer’s excellent performance in
NLP, ViT [2] represents the first successful application of
Transformers in image processing, capturing the global context
information of input images through multiple cascaded Trans-
former layers. This contributes to the successful adoption of
Transformers in image classification. Subsequently, Chen et al.
[24] introduced TransUNet, marking a new era for Trans-
formers in medical image segmentation. Since TransUNet
directly adopts the Transformer network from NLP for image
segmentation, the input image patch size is fixed, resulting
in high computational overhead. To address this limitation,
Valanarasu et al. [25] proposed MedT, which incorporates
a gating mechanism that can be automatically adjusted to
obtain position embedding weights suitable for datasets of
varying sizes. Recognizing the greater complexity and higher
resolution of images compared to text, Cao et al. [12] proposed
Swin-Unet, a pure Transformer network for medical image
segmentation that incorporates the shifted window multihead
SA (SW-MSA) mechanism from Swin Transformer [26].
Swin-Unet achieved state-of-the-art (SOTA) segmentation per-
formance on the Synapse and ACDC multiorgan segmentation
datasets upon its release. To better leverage Transformers for
processing dermoscopic image data, Wang et al. [13] designed
BAT, a network based on boundary detection principles. The
proposed boundary-wise attention gate (BAG) fully utilizes
prior knowledge of image boundaries to capture finer details
in medical images. BAT achieved impressive Dice scores on
skin lesion datasets, surpassing many existing medical image
segmentation networks.
In contrast to the SA mechanisms found in existing Trans-
former architectures [12], [13], our (S)W-ACAM requires
fewer parameters and lower computational cost while effec-
tively capturing global cross-dimensional long-range depen-
dencies in medical images and enhancing the global feature
expression capability of the segmentation network.
Our DCTC-Net, while eliminating the need for pretraining
on large labeled datasets, effectively preserves local details
and global semantic information in medical images. Specifi-
cally, DCTC-Net leverages DDConv to capture complex local
features often obscured by noise or variability, while (S)W-
ACAM leverages global context to improve segmentation
accuracy and robustness across different anatomical structures.
These benefits are demonstrated in the ablation study and
through competitive quantitative results on the ISIC2018,
LiTS-Liver, and ACDC datasets.
III. METHOD
A. Overall Architecture
The fusion of local and global features significantly
enhances performance in medical image segmentation. CNNs
capture local features within medical images through con-
volution operations and hierarchical feature representation.
Conversely, Transformers achieve the extraction of global
features in medical images through cascaded SA mechanisms
and contextual matrix operations. To fully leverage both local
details and global semantic features present in medical images,
we propose a parallel interactive network architecture: DCTC-
Net. The overall architecture of the network is illustrated in
Fig. 2(a). DCTC-Net capitalizes on the complementary proper-
ties of CNNs and Transformers. During forward propagation,
DCTC-Net continuously feeds local details extracted by the
CNN branch to the decoder of the Transformer branch. Simi-
larly, DCTC-Net also feeds the global long-range relationships
captured by the Transformer branch to the decoder of the
CNN branch. Consequently, the proposed DCTC-Net provides
superior local and global feature representation compared to
pure CNN or Transformer networks, showcasing significant
potential in the field of medical image segmentation.
Specifically, DCTC-Net comprises four key components:
a patch embedding module, a dynamically adaptive CNN

extract these multiscale change targets. Similarly, the cross-

main stages. By employing (S)W-ACAM attention in each
stage, as illustrated in Fig. 2(b), the segmentation network
gains enhanced understanding of the global dependencies
within medical images. This allows it to effectively capture
positional information between different organs and improve
the separability between foreground and background.

B. Dynamic Deformable Convolution
Vanilla convolution exhibits spatial invariance and channel
specificity, which limits its capacity to adapt to varying visual
patterns across different spatial locations. Moreover, the fixed
receptive field of vanilla convolution makes it challenging to
effectively extract features from small targets or those with
blurred edges. Consequently, vanilla convolution inherently
possesses limited adaptability and weak generalization ability
for complex feature representation in medical images. While
existing deformable convolution [22] and dynamic convolu-
tion approaches [20] outperform vanilla convolution to some
extent, they still face the problem of balancing network
performance and model size when applied to medical image
segmentation.
To overcome the aforementioned shortcomings of current
convolution operations, this article introduces a novel con-
volution strategy termed DDConv, illustrated in Fig. 3. As
shown, DDConv adaptively learns kernel deformation offsets
and weight coefficients based on the specific task and data
distribution, thereby changing both the shapes and values of
convolution kernels. It can effectively address the problems
of large data distribution differences and significant target
deformation encountered in medical image segmentation. Fur-
thermore, DDConv is designed as a plug-and-play module,
readily embeddable within any network structure.
The shape transformation of the convolutional kernel in
DDConv is based on the network’s learning of deformation
offsets. The segmentation network initially samples the input
feature map X using a square convolutional kernel S , and then
performs a weighted summation with a weight matrix M. The
square convolution kernel S defines the extent of the receptive
field; for example, a 3 × 3 convolution kernel can be expressed

blurred boundaries in medical images.

C. Shifted Window Adaptive Complementary Attention
Module
The SA mechanism, a core component of Transform-
ers, captures long-range dependencies within feature maps
through matrix operations. However, SA mechanisms primar-
ily focus on dependencies in the spatial dimension, neglecting
cross-dimensional dependencies between spatial and channel
features [29]. As a result, when applied to medical image
segmentation characterized by low-contrast and high-density
noise, SA mechanisms may struggle to distinguish segmen-
tation targets from the background, leading to suboptimal
performance.
To address the aforementioned limitations, we introduce a
novel cross-dimensional SA module termed (S)W-ACAM. As
illustrated in Fig. 4, (S)W-ACAM consists of four parallel
branches: the top two branches implement a conventional
dual attention module, while the bottom two branches are
cross-dimensional attention modules. Compared to existing SA
modules, such as spatial SA, channel SA, and dual SA, our
proposed (S)W-ACAM can not only effectively extract long-
range dependencies in both spatial and channel dimensions,
but also capture the cross-dimensional long-range dependen-
cies between spatial and channel features. The four branches
are designed to be complementary and enhance the sepa-
rability between foreground and background. Therefore, the
four branches together provide richer long-range dependence
relationships, ultimately improving segmentation results for
medical images.
The standard Transformer architecture [2] employs global
SA, calculating the relationship between one token and all
other tokens, resulting in high computational cost. This com-
plexity increases exponentially with image size. To improve
computational efficiency, we adopt the shifted window cal-
culation method, inspired by Swin Transformer [26], which
restricts SA calculations to local windows. However, directly
applying the shifted window method to our four-branch
(S)W-ACAM module does not sufficiently reduce the overall
computational complexity. Therefore, we also introduce com-
pact convolutional projection. First, we reduce the spatial size
of the medical image through the shifted window operation.
Second, we compress the channel dimension of feature maps
using compact convolutional projection. Finally, we perform
the SA calculation. Notably, this method not only better cap-
tures global high-dimensional information in medical images
but also significantly reduces the computational costs. With

of size M × M, the complexity of the (S)W-ACAM, global
MSA in the original Transformer, and the (S)W-MSA in Swin

IV. EXPERIMENT AND RESULTS
A. Datasets
We conducted research on the skin lesion segmentation
dataset (ISIC2018) from the International Symposium on
Biomedical Imaging (ISBI) [49], the Liver Tumor Segmen-
tation (LiTS) Challenge dataset from the Medical Image
Computing and Computer Assisted Intervention (MICCAI)
Society [50], and the Automated Cardiac Diagnosis Chal-
lenge (ACDC) dataset from the University Hospital of Dijon
(France) [51]. These three datasets have different data types
and data distributions. The ISIC2018 dataset has electron
microscope images, which contain 2594 dermoscopic images
for training, but the ground truth images of the testing set have
not been released, so we performed a fivefold cross-validation
on the training set for a fair comparison. The LiTS dataset is
a CT image of the human abdomen, containing 131 3-D CT
liver scans, of which 100 scans are used for training, and the
remaining 31 scans are used for testing. The ACDC dataset is
an MRI image of the human heart, which contains cardiac
short-axis Cine MRI data from 100 patients. The ACDC
dataset includes healthy patients and patients with previous
myocardial infarction, dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic
cardiomyopathy, and abnormal right ventricle (20 scans for
each group). These data are obtained over a period of six
years using two MRI scanners of two magnetic strengths (1.5
and 3.0 T). In addition, all images are empirically resized to
224 × 224 for efficiency.
B. Implementation Details and Evaluation Metrics
All networks are implemented using PyTorch 1.7 on one
NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090 GPU with 24 GB memory. For
optimization, we used the Adam optimizer with an initial
learning rate of 0.001. The learning rate is dynamically
adjusted during training: it is halved if the validation loss
does not decrease for ten consecutive epochs. To train the
networks, we employed a combination of mean squared error
(mse) loss and dice loss. As for other hyperparameter settings,
we primarily followed the settings of mainstream methods in
the field of medical image segmentation, while adapting them
to our DCTC-Net and datasets. This is based on reports that
CPFNet [33] and Swin-Unet [12] work well with such settings.
Specifically, our total epoch number is set to 200.

Sensitivity (SE), Accuracy (AC), and Specificity (SP). In the
experiment on the LiTS-Liver dataset, we conducted an overall
evaluation of mainstream medical image segmentation net-
works using five metrics: DI, VOE, RVD, ASD, and RMSD.
In the experiment on the ACDC dataset, we conducted an

C. Evaluation and Results
In this article, we conducted a comprehensive comparison
across three multimodal datasets (ISIC2018, LiTS-Liver, and
ACDC) between the proposed DCTC-Net and a range of
mainstream medical image segmentation networks, including
U-Net [1], Attention Unet [31], Swin-Unet [12], BRAU-Net++
[39], VM-Unet [45], and TMU [48], which possess inherent
encoder–decoder structures. To ensure a fair comparison with
CvT [36], PVT [37], and CrossForm [38], architectures lacking
the decoder component, we augmented them with a decoder
mirroring the structure of DCTC-Net’s decoder to enable fair
segmentation performance comparisons. Beyond presenting
quantitative results, we provide a detailed analysis of the
factors contributing to DCTC-Net’s performance.
Table II shows the quantitative comparison between the pro-
posed DCTC-Net and current mainstream CNNs, Transformer,
and Mamba networks on the ISIC2018 dataset. From the
experimental results, we conclude that our DCTC-Net achieves
an optimal balance between model size and performance: it
achieves the highest dice score of 91.23% without pretraining,
while DCTC-Net-T delivers the lowest computational cost
at just 4.53 GFLOPs and 11.58 M parameters, achieving a
competitive dice score of 90.72%. While U-Mamba demon-
strates some effectiveness, it achieves a lower dice score of
87.54% with a high computational cost of 453.89 GFLOPs.
Our DCTC-Net-B network outperforms BAT, CvT, and Cross-
Form, with dice scores 1.02%, 3.00%, and 3.79% higher,
respectively, while maintaining a lower parameter count. These
superior results can be attributed to DCTC-Net’s ability to
address the specific challenges posed by dermoscopic images:
blurred lesion boundaries, complex textures, and interfering
factors such as hair and blood vessels. The DDConv module,
with its ability to adaptively adjust the shape and weights of
convolution kernels, is better equipped to capture the subtle
structures and textures of lesion boundaries. Similarly, the
(S)W-ACAM module effectively models the global contextual
information in images, suppressing noise interference and
helping the model to better distinguish between lesions and
background.
Table III presents the quantitative comparison of DCTC-
Net’s performance on the LiTS-Liver dataset, alongside
established mainstream segmentation networks. The results
highlight DCTC-Net’s superior performance in medical image
segmentation, further confirming its ability to preserve both
local and global features in medical images. Notably, DCTC-
Net-B achieves SOTA performance with a dice score of
96.82 ± 1.22%, while DCTC-Net-T demonstrates a com-
petitive second-best dice score of 96.48 ± 1.05%, both
with considerably fewer parameters (21.24 M and 11.58 M,
respectively) than several prominent methods. Furthermore,
DCTC-Net-B demonstrates superior performance in VOE and
all distance metrics compared to Mamba methods. These
achievements can be attributed to the architecture’s ability to
address the unique challenges of liver segmentation: irregular
liver and tumor shapes, low image contrast, and numerous
small tumors. By adaptively adjusting the shape of its convolu-
tional kernels, the DDConv module is particularly well-suited
to segmenting the irregular contours of liver tumors. The
dual-branch design, which fuses local and global information,
provides greater robustness to noise and contrast variations,
enabling the model to accurately infer tumor location and
shape even in low-contrast regions.
Table IV shows the quantitative comparison of DCTC-
Net’s performance on the ACDC dataset alongside recent
prominent medical image segmentation networks. The results
demonstrate DCTC-Net’s competitive performance in MRI-
based multiorgan segmentation. DCTC-Net-B achieves an
average DI score of 91.95 ± 0.66%, and the lowest average
95HD of 3.27 ± 0.51 mm. Although DCTC-Net-T and DCTC-
Net-B do not achieve the best 95HD scores, they achieve
superior average DI scores on this challenging dataset; sig-
nificantly outperforming U-Mamba’s dice score of 90.73 ±
0.76%. Notably, DCTC-Net’s design specifically addresses the
key challenges of cardiac MRI analysis, including dynamic
motion, complex tissue structures, and interpatient variabil-
ity. The dual-branch structure enables effective capture of
both global cardiac anatomy and fine-grained tissue details,
while the learned discriminative features better accommodate
interpatient differences in heart morphology. The proposed
method also outperforms other SOTA methods, such as
the Transformer-based methods like MCAFT-Net (91.16 ±
0.79% dice score) and Mamba-based methods like DMA-Net
(91.22 ± 0.71% dice score) used for comparison.

D. Failure Case Analysis
While DCTC-Net demonstrates robust performance across
various datasets, analyzing its limitations is essential for
guiding future research. We present typical failure cases in
Fig. 7 to provide a comprehensive evaluation.
Fig. 7 illustrates two primary failure modes. The first two
rows show instances of inadequate handling of blurred bound-
aries. In these examples, the target objects exhibit extremely
low contrast with surrounding tissue, and boundaries are
inherently ambiguous. Our DCTC-Net, while attempting to
delineate regions, produces contours that are either discon-
tinuous or slightly inaccurate. This highlights a challenge in
scenarios with very low signal-to-noise ratios, where pre-
cise edge determination is difficult even for human experts.
Future work could address this by incorporating advanced
boundary-aware mechanisms, such as explicit boundary pre-
diction branches during training or loss functions that heavily
penalize boundary errors.
The bottom two rows of Fig. 7 display cases of missed
small target detection. Here, DCTC-Net fails to identify very
small-scale lesions. A likely cause is the hierarchical down-
sampling process in the encoder. During successive pooling
or strided convolution operations, feature information corre-
sponding to tiny targets can be significantly diluted or lost
entirely, making accurate decoder reconstruction challenging.
To mitigate this issue, future improvements could focus on
optimizing network architecture to better preserve small-scale
features, for example, by modifying downsampling strategies,
enhancing cross-scale feature fusion using techniques like
feature pyramid networks (FPNs), or designing loss functions

V. DISCUSSION
Despite exhibiting promising performance in medical image
segmentation tasks, DCTC-Net, like all deep learning models,
faces certain limitations in specific application scenarios. To
comprehensively evaluate DCTC-Net’s applicability and guide
future research, we examine its limitations concerning data
dependence, computational resource requirements, and gener-
alization ability.
First, our DCTC-Net relies on a substantial amount of
labeled data for training. While techniques such as the Gaus-
sian ramp-up curve are employed to mitigate the small sample
problem, DCTC-Net may still face the risk of overfitting
when available labeled data is severely limited, leading to
degraded generalization on unseen data. This limitation is
particularly prominent in scenarios involving rare diseases or
novel medical imaging modalities, where obtaining sufficient
labeled data is often challenging.
Second, the dual-branch structure and use of Transformer
modules in DCTC-Net contribute to a relatively high param-
eter count and computational complexity. Although we have
optimized the model’s efficiency, deploying DCTC-Net and
performing real-time inference may still present challenges to
devices with limited computational resources, such as embed-
ded medical devices or mobile diagnostic platforms. There-
fore, further reducing DCTC-Net’s computational complexity
in resource-constrained scenarios is a noteworthy research
direction.
Third, DCTC-Net’s performance is highly dependent on
the distribution of the training data. We have primarily val-
idated the effectiveness of DCTC-Net on datasets such as
ISIC2018, LiTS-Liver, and ACDC, which encompass various
medical imaging modalities such as dermoscopic images, CT
images, and MRI images. However, DCTC-Net’s performance
requires further validation for other types of medical images.
Furthermore, domain shift, arising from differences in imag-
ing parameters, image contrast, and noise levels, may exist
between different datasets. Enhancing DCTC-Net’s general-
ization ability across various data types and domains for future
research.
Future research can focus on several aspects to enhance
DCTC-Net’s applicability and robustness. First, weakly super-
vised learning methods, such as semi-supervised learning [55],
[56] and self-supervised learning [57], could be explored
to reduce the reliance on labeled data. Second, techniques
like model compression [58] and knowledge distillation [59]
can be employed to further reduce computational complexity.
Third, domain adaptation learning methods [60] should be
investigated to improve generalization across different medical
image types and domains.
We believe that our DCTC-Net will play a crucial role in
the field of medical image segmentation. Through continuous
exploration and improvement, it will provide enhanced support
for clinical diagnosis and treatment.
VI. CONCLUSION
In this study, we proposed a novel architecture, DCTC-Net,
which combines dynamically adaptive CNNs and a cross-
dimensional fusion Transformer in a parallel configuration
for medical image segmentation. The proposed DCTC-Net
integrates the strengths of both CNNs and Transformers,
preserving both local details and global semantic features
of medical images by leveraging local relationship modeling
and long-range dependence modeling. The proposed DDConv
overcomes the limitations of fixed receptive fields and param-
eter sharing inherent in vanilla convolution, improving local
feature representation and enabling adaptive extraction of
spatial features. The proposed (S)W-ACAM SA mechanism
comprehensively captures the cross-dimensional correlation
between spatial features and channels, and adaptively learns
salient information between spatial and channel dimensions
through network training. Furthermore, by replacing the MLP
in traditional Transformers with LPM, DCTC-Net significantly

While DCTC-Net has demonstrated encouraging results,
several directions warrant further exploration. First, although
the model performs well across multiple modalities, its
generalization on broader medical image types, such as
ultrasound and digital pathology, remains to be validated;
incorporating domain adaptation techniques will be cru-
cial. Second, to meet deployment demands on extremely

explored to optimize efficiency further. Finally, integrating
task-specific prior knowledge, such as anatomical shape con-
straints, could enhance segmentation robustness in complex

valuable for clinical applications.
